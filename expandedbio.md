As a senior undergraduate at UNC, I have had many opportunities to engage in ecological and geographic work. I won fifth place in the GIS Day Map Competition during the 2024 - 2025 school year with a map of Chicago, IL food deserts (included in this portfolio); I assisted with DNA extraction from cyanobacterial mats for the Sophie McCoy lab in 2025; and I participated in the UNC Outer Banks Field Site (OBXFS) during the fall semester of 2025, where I completed 17 hours of credit, including an internship with the Atlantic Marine Energy Center and developed a capstone project with my cohort on the unique maritime forest biome situated in the area. With the Atlantic Marine Energy Center, I was lucky enough to collect acoustic background data from Jennette's Pier, a local North Carolina aquarium hub, process the data, and then design an exhibit in collaboration with the Aquarium to be installed in the lobby of the Pier. In addition to designing the exhibit, I was the sole creator of the maps for the 2025 OBXFS capstone report, which totaled five (also linked in this portfolio). Our final report totaled over 100 pages and our presentation was given to over 100 audience members, which included prominent community members and UNC environmental professionals. During this most recent summer, I split my time between interning with the Albemarle-Pamlico National Estuarine Partnership (APNEP) and pursuing the beginnings of my honors thesis project. For APNEP, I crafted the graphic design elements for the Targeted Protection and Restoration Near-Term Implementation Plan (TPR NTIP) and facilitated a workshop for influential coastal education and outreach professionals. At the same time, I began the research on my honors thesis. I collected field data at five distinct geographic locations along the southern Outer Banks, cleaned and analyzed the data in R, and compiled the results into a poster that I have presented twice now. I found support for my hypothesis that when data is being collected in the field, coastal researchers are more likely to be finding widgeon grass as opposed to shoal grass. They are recorded as the same in the field due to their morphological indistinguishability, but based on the environmental parameters I encountered, I believe that widgeon grass is more likely to be growing in the region than shoal grass. I have now begun to apply my skills to my expanded honors thesis project, which is the creation of a habitat suitability model for submerged aquatic vegetation (SAV) in the Outer Banks, which has been a delightful challenge. I have learned how to spatially interpolate environmental variable data (i.e. salinity, temperature, etc.) using the kriging method and stack these layers together with statistical reasoning to craft a predictive model for the most suitable habitat for each of the three SAV species I am treating. I hope to eventually publish this work and send it to the coastal conservation organizations that I have built relationships with, including APNEP, the Division of Marine Fisheries, East Carolina University's Coastal Studies Institute, and UNC's Institute of Marine Science. <br>
I hope to work in geographic analysis of ecological parameters as a career. I find the intersection between spatial elements and ecological parameters fascinating, and I believe that we need to understand spatial relationships as thoroughly as we do the ecological concepts to better inform species conservation.
